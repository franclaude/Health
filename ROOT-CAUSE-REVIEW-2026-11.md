## 1a. When not to wait

Nothing in this review replaces being examined. Call 999 or go to A&E the same day (or ring NHS 111 if unsure) for any of these, even if it feels like the usual illness:

- Weakness that is clearly worse over hours or days; new weakness of one side of the body or face, or of both legs; trouble swallowing or choking; slurred speech; double vision.
- New numbness between the legs or around the back passage, new difficulty passing urine, or loss of bladder or bowel control.
- Breathlessness that suddenly worsens or stops you lying flat; new morning headaches, unusual drowsiness or confusion. These can mean carbon dioxide is building up and are more likely after sedating medicines.
- New chest pain that is heavy or spreads to the arm or jaw; fainting.
- Fever with a severe headache and stiff neck, or a new rash that does not fade when pressed.
- A blood pressure reading of 180/120 or higher, or a high reading with severe headache, chest pain or loss of vision.
- Thoughts of ending your life, or feeling unable to keep yourself safe. Samaritans answer at any hour on 116 123, and NHS 111 has a mental health option.

New loss of strength needs a hands-on examination (strength muscle by muscle, reflexes, sensation) soon, even without the features above.

### Why I think the order of events is clear

- The heterophile ("glandular fever") test usually turns positive around the day symptoms begin and fades within 3–12 months (Dunmire 2015). It misses about a quarter of cases in the first week.
- A negative-to-positive switch inside 11 days, with lymphocytes tripling and then hepatitis and exudative tonsillitis, is the expected sequence of a first EBV infection.
- Counting the 32–49 day incubation back from an onset between late December and mid-January puts infection at roughly mid-November to mid-December 2016.
- Chills from late December and a sore throat in early January came two to three weeks before the illness declared itself. That is longer than the usual lead-in of a few days to two weeks. They may have been a long lead-in or a separate winter infection; the record cannot tell these apart.

## 6. Re-reading specific tests

### The treadmill test of 2 February 2022

You asked that this not be misread. Here is what the breath-by-breath record shows and does not show.

- It was a Bruce protocol: 2.7 km/h at 10% gradient for 3 minutes, then 4.0 km/h at 12%, stopped at 5 min 20 s for breathlessness. You were on ivabradine 7.5 mg. Heart rate at the end was 130–131, 67–68% of predicted. It was a short, submaximal test.
- At rest on the mask, end-tidal (breath) CO2 was 4.4–5.0 kPa, which is not raised.
- In the first 40 seconds of walking it rose to 5.2–5.7 kPa while you were breathing 11–27 times a minute. A rise at the start of exercise is usual.
- From about one minute into walking there were runs of very slow, very deep breaths: 7–12 a minute, about 2 litres each, with single breaths of 3.29 litres. The highest end-tidal readings (6.5 and 7.0 kPa) came on those largest breaths. End-tidal CO2 over-reads blood CO2 on large breaths (Jones 1979).
- In the second stage you mostly breathed 14–30 times a minute and end-tidal CO2 stayed at 5.4–6.5 kPa.
- The one coherent blood sample, at the end of exercise, was 5.66 kPa. That is upper-normal, and it had not fallen despite a lactate of 5.4. That absent fall is what the specialist called relative hypoventilation.
- So the test documents the breathing pattern you describe: slow, very deep breaths with holds. It cannot show why you were breathing that way, and your explanation (the sensation in your chest) is consistent with it. It also cannot separate that from a blunted drive to breathe, which was the specialist's first suggestion, or from a test that stopped before the point at which breathing normally speeds up.
- Single breaths of 3.3 litres (two thirds of your vital capacity) make severe weakness of the breathing muscles on that day unlikely. They do not exclude mild weakness or weakness of the diaphragm alone, which shows when lying flat. The specialist reached the same view ("probably less likely") and still planned a sleep assessment, an alpha-glucosidase blood test and diaphragm tests. None of those results is in the repository.
- The resting blood CO2 of 6.20 kPa was logged 24 seconds into the recording. The laboratory printed a gap of 1.46 kPa above the breath reading, which is not physiological with normal lungs. Either the blood was taken at a different moment from the breaths it is compared with, in which case the value may have been real for that moment, or the sample was mishandled. ## 7. The explanations compared

For each explanation I asked what it predicts, what it explains and what contradicts it. The words in the headings are my judgement, not measurements. They are not shares of 100%: more than one explanation can be true at once.

### A. Post-infectious syndrome: EBV in 2017, SARS-CoV-2 in 2020 (most likely overall description)

- **Explains:** the first step directly after documented EBV; chills and cold limbs after 2017; reported relapse after exertion; standing tachycardia; normal inflammatory and autoimmune tests; flares that you report following infections.
- **Supported by:** the known 9–12% rate of lasting illness after glandular fever. Anxiety, bowel symptoms and asthma each modestly raise the risk of lasting illness after COVID (hazard ratios 1.15–1.35 in 486,149 people, Subramanian 2022); allergic rhinitis has an odds ratio near 2 at very low certainty (Wolff 2023).
- **Weak point:** lasting post-COVID illness is expected to begin within about three months of infection. Yours began six to eight months after March 2020. The antibody result of 1 December 2020 shows infection at some time before that date, not when. So this explanation needs either a second, untested infection in autumn 2020 or a different virus as the trigger.
- **It is a diagnosis of exclusion.** Its likelihood depends on exclusions that have not been done (section 9).
- **Four treatments you tried have since failed in controlled trials:** nirmatrelvir (three trials, up to 25 days), a monoclonal antibody (a different one from yours), low-dose naltrexone (preprint) and ivabradine. Your non-responses neither confirm nor refute this explanation.
- **Does not explain:** the belching and chest "brace" in detail; the raised base excess; ALT and IgA; the 2026 blood pressure.
- **Honest gap:** no study has tested whether prior glandular fever or prior post-viral illness predicts a worse course after COVID.

### B. A hidden infection running since May 2016 (very unlikely)

- **Predicts:** raised inflammatory markers at some point, falling blood counts, organ damage, a positive organism test.
- **Found:** none of these in ten years. EBV DNA, hepatitis B and C, HIV (2019), four other viruses by PCR (2021) and validated tick-borne tests are negative.
- **Not excluded:** infection confined to tissue, which blood tests cannot see.
- **Would reopen it:** fever, drenching night sweats, unplanned weight loss, a gland that grows, or a blood count or CRP that becomes abnormal between flares.

### C. No ongoing immune process: anxiety, medication effects, deconditioning and the changed breathing pattern (real contributors; not sufficient alone)

- **Explains part:** months mostly in bed lower heart mass, blood volume and exercise capacity and cause standing tachycardia. Amitriptyline raises heart rate; a hospital team said so in December 2020. The breathing pattern contributes to the CO2 readings.
- **Does not obviously explain:** relapse lasting more than a day after exertion; the stepwise course after infections. Nobody disputes the two infections themselves; the question is what kept symptoms going.
- **Literature:** in prospective glandular fever cohorts, prior psychiatric history did not predict a lasting fatigue syndrome but did predict a lasting mood disorder (Hickie 2006; White 2001). One cohort disagrees: in Pedersen 2019 (200 adolescents) baseline anxiety and pain predicted fatigue at six months. Sleep anxiety, treated with amitriptyline, dates from 2014. So this literature supports an anxiety component for the 2017–2019 step without making it the whole explanation.
- **Medication, beyond heart rate.** You took amitriptyline from December 2014 until at least October 2023, at doses between about 20 and 40 mg. Three effects deserve checking against your own dates (general pharmacology, not re-checked in this review):
  - It increases the effect of alcohol.
  - Lowering, missing or stopping doses can cause chills, nausea, diarrhoea, anxiety and poor sleep. The record shows dose changes near several episodes: repeat requests at three of the five May 2016 visits, a cut to 20 mg on 7 July 2016, a rise to 30–40 mg in February–March 2017 and 20 mg again by October 2017. It cannot explain chills during the years on a steady prescription of 30 mg (2018–2021).
  - It slows the gullet, stomach and bladder and reduces sweating.
- **Autumn 2026:** three drug changes coincided with your decline: sertraline being withdrawn, quetiapine 50 mg and atenolol 50 mg. Each can cause dizziness, tiredness or weakness. This is a reason to review them with the prescriber, not to stop them.
- **Fair conclusion:** these are layers on top. Months mostly in bed will reduce strength, so disuse is a likely contributor to the loss of strength since autumn 2026. But the records cannot tell me whether the weakness followed the bed rest or led to it. On the reassuring side, the November 2026 examination recorded limb tone and strength as preserved and CK was 58. On the other side, reflexes were not tested, and the muscle antibody panel, brain and spine MRI and breathing-muscle tests have never been done.

### D. A recurrent-fever (autoinflammatory) disease (very unlikely)

- **For:** childhood "recurring temperatures" at age 2–4 with a normal kidney work-up in April 1996; relapses every 10–14 days in 2021; raised IgA; the mouth ulcers you report.
- **Against:** in these diseases CRP rises in essentially every attack (Korkmaz 2002) and yours has never been raised outside two infections; your flares are without fever; colchicine and six weeks of prednisolone did not help.
- **Deciding test:** CRP and serum amyloid A on the worst day of a flare, twice.

### E. Dermatomyositis-spectrum disease (unlikely, but never tested)

- **For:** your sister. A full sibling carries about 2.5 times the background risk, roughly 0.4% absolute (Che 2021). Hand and wrist pain, redness over elbows, knees and knuckles, the mouth ulcers you report, and the loss of strength you report. Normal CK and negative ANA do not exclude the anti-MDA5 type. New anti-MDA5 cases rose sharply in Yorkshire after COVID (David 2024).
- **Against:** the rash is intermittent, painless and not itchy; lung CT was normal in December 2022, when 42–80% of anti-MDA5 cases have lung disease, usually early; ALT has been raised since July 2016, before any skin symptoms; the one paired value (ALT 43, AST 23) and CK of 73 and 58 do not suggest muscle as the source; strength was preserved on examination.
- **Unconfirmed:** your sister's diagnosis appears in your notes only second-hand ("what he thinks is dermatomyositis" in 2017; "has a diagnosis of dermatomyositis" in 2021). Her confirmed diagnosis and antibody type, if she is willing to share them, would sharpen this.
- **Never tested:** your screens contained only two myositis-specific antibodies (Jo-1 and Mi-2), plus PM-Scl and Ro52. MDA5, TIF1-gamma, NXP2, SAE and others have not been looked for.
- **Caution:** weak bands on these panels are usually false; anti-MDA5 was the commonest false positive in one 2025 study (10.6% of controls). Only a strong, repeatable result with a matching rash would count.

### F. Behçet's disease or spondyloarthritis (each unlikely)

- **Behçet's:** from the records alone only the pustular skin lesions count (1 point on the international criteria; 4 are required). If recurrent mouth ulcers are confirmed by a clinician the score would be 3 (point weights from memory). No genital ulcers, eye inflammation or clots are recorded.
- **Spondyloarthritis or SAPHO:** severe acne, pilonidal and perianal disease, a father with colitis, chest-wall tightness and spinal stiffness fit. A normal 2016 MRI of the sacroiliac joints, negative HLA-B27, no response to anti-inflammatories and stiffness that comes with flares, not at night, argue against.

### G. A nervous-system or balance-system cause (unlikely for the structural causes, but never looked for)

- No brain or spinal MRI report appears in the repository. The only MRI reports are the August 2016 pelvis and hips and the heart. If one has been done, disregard this.
- Glandular fever roughly doubles the later risk of multiple sclerosis (relative risk 2.17, Handel 2010). For hospital-diagnosed illness at age 20–24 the hazard ratio was 1.89, falling to a non-significant 1.51 when compared with siblings (Xu 2021). The absolute risk remains low.
- Several of your symptoms overlap: a locked or braced feeling in the chest, overheating with stress or meals, eyeball pain, trouble following movement, facial pain, burning sensations, and worsening with infections.
- Against: relapse every 10–14 days and relapse after exertion are not typical; no abnormal neurological signs are recorded, though the only documented examination did not test reflexes.
- A crowded skull base (Chiari malformation) can also produce dizziness, swallowing and breathing-control symptoms (from memory).
- A normal MRI of the brain and whole spine would make multiple sclerosis and Chiari malformation very unlikely. Small incidental spots are common on brain scans in young adults and would need a neurologist's reading, not alarm.
- A commoner explanation for constant non-spinning dizziness, intolerance of visual motion and light and noise sensitivity is a disorder of the balance system or a migraine-type disorder (general clinical knowledge, not re-checked). These are assessed by a neuro-otologist from the history and balance tests, not by MRI. No hearing or balance test is in the repository.

### Which is best supported

Explanation A accounts for the most with the fewest added assumptions, and C describes real layers on top of it. B, D, E, F and the structural causes in G are each unlikely. But E, G, the oesophageal question and the breathing-control question have never been properly tested. For each there is a test that would move the question a long way: a clearly normal result would make the condition very unlikely, and an abnormal one would need a specialist to interpret.

## 8. So what is the immune change, and how could it be looked for?

What the evidence supports:

- Nothing immunological is demonstrable before late 2016. You were very likely EBV-naive until then.
- Since January 2017 you carry latent EBV, as about 95% of adults do.
- By your account, deteriorations follow infections or exertion and begin with a warning sign. In your written history that is an eye change or a red follicular bump, then worse breathing over days, then weeks of worse symptoms with incomplete recovery. In your message to me it was a stiff back and neck, then mouth ulcers, then the chest symptoms. The 2021 letters add neck and back stiffness. This is what you report; the records do not demonstrate the sequence.
- Whether your immune response itself is larger than normal has never been measured. On the two documented infection days (January 2017, January 2018) CRP and blood counts behaved in the ordinary way.
- Between flares, and on the dates that happened to be sampled, the measurable immune system is normal.

Where the science stands (2024–2026), so that you know what is and is not established:

| Proposed mechanism | Best human evidence | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Persisting viral antigen | Antigen found in 43% with core symptoms against 21% without (Swank 2024) | Replicated but non-specific; antiviral trials negative |
| Complement activation | Two cohorts; discriminates modestly (Cervia-Hasler 2024; Baillie 2024) | Direction replicated; no treatment trial |
| Autoantibodies | Patient antibodies transferred to mice produce pain behaviour in three laboratories | Replicated for pain only; antibody-removal trials negative |
| EBV reactivation | Antibody footprints in three cohorts; EBV in blood at the time of acute COVID in one (Su 2022); not found in blood during long COVID (Klein 2023) | Association only |
| Low blood return to the heart, poor oxygen extraction | Invasive exercise studies from one research network | Not independently replicated |
| Small-fibre nerve damage | 10 of 16 biopsies abnormal in one referral series | Uncontrolled |
| Low cortisol, low serotonin, brain inflammation on PET | Initial reports | Not reproduced or inconsistent |
| Persistent interferon excess | Raised at 8 months, largely resolved by 24 months | No evidence of persistence beyond that |

A central (brain-stem or hypothalamic) disorder of the "sickness response" is a reasonable hypothesis for chills without fever. A study of seven patients found normal 24-hour core temperature. In adolescents, Wyller 2007 found slightly higher resting temperature, catecholamines and heart rate with abnormal responses to local cooling, which points to sympathetic control of skin blood vessels. It remains a hypothesis with no validated test.

**A test of your hypothesis that has never been tried.** Your central claim is that your immune system over-reacts to viruses. Every blood test you have had was timed by appointment, not by flare. Bloods taken through your GP or clinic at the first warning sign you recognise, and again in a settled week, would at least show whether anything measurable moves:

- CRP and serum amyloid A.
- Full blood count with differential, ferritin, CK.
- A respiratory virus PCR swab (including SARS-CoV-2) each time, to show whether a virus was present at all.
- An interferon read-out if a centre offers one. An accredited flow-cytometry marker (SIGLEC1/CD169 on monocytes) exists in at least one European laboratory; these are specialist tests, not validated for this purpose.
- A swab of a fresh mouth ulcer for herpes simplex PCR, and a clinician or dated photograph to document the ulcers.

How to read it, honestly:

- A flat result on all of these during a typical flare would make an interleukin-type or interferon-type surge in the blood unlikely as the driver of that flare. It would not show where the abnormality is, and it does not test complement, autoantibody or tissue-level mechanisms.
- A raised result is harder to read. Any ordinary viral infection raises CRP modestly and raises interferon markers in everyone, so a raised value on a day you have caught a virus shows a response, not an over-response.
- The cleanest test is a flare that follows exertion with a negative virus swab.
- Six weeks of prednisolone from 30 mg, colchicine and three months of a product sold as a JAK inhibitor changed nothing. That is weak evidence against a steroid-responsive inflammatory process, weak because doses and the last product are uncertain.

## 11. What would change my conclusions

- An EBV antibody result or archived serum from 2016 showing infection before December 2016 would overturn sections 1–3.
- Weakness found on examination, abnormal reflexes, a raised CK or abnormal nerve or muscle tests would put a nerve, muscle or spinal cause ahead of explanation A.
- A raised resting or overnight CO2 on a properly taken sample, or low breathing-muscle pressures, would do the same.
- Blood pressure confirmed as raised on home or 24-hour monitoring, especially with low potassium or an abnormal aldosterone and renin result, would point to a separate and treatable cause.
- Fever, drenching night sweats, unplanned weight loss, a gland that grows, or a blood count or CRP that becomes abnormal between flares would reopen explanation B and need prompt investigation.
- A clear and lasting change, better or worse, after a medicine is started, stopped or changed would point to a drug effect.
- A raised CRP, amyloid A or interferon marker in a flare without a virus on the swab would move the weight towards a specific immune pathway.
- An abnormal manometry, MRI, biopsy histology or strong myositis antibody would each introduce a second diagnosis alongside, or instead of, explanation A.
- Tests done elsewhere that are not in this repository could change any of the above.
